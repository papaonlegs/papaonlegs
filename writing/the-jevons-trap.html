4 — The Jevons trap: efficient AI, hungrier planet

Part of Tokenmaxxing · Farouk Umar

Google's own 2026 environmental report says its AI got 44x more efficient per prompt in a year. The same report says its electricity use grew 37% that year. Both numbers are true, and they're not in tension — they're the same phenomenon.

This is Jevons paradox, not a contradiction: efficiency gains per unit of use drive down the cost of using it, which drives up total use, and total use outgrows the efficiency gain. Researchers Luccioni, Strubell, and Crawford named this explicitly for AI at FAccT 2025 — and the market backed the theory before the paper did: Nvidia shipped over a million more GPUs in 2024 than in 2023, despite — or because of — real per-chip efficiency gains in that same window. Cheaper per query doesn't mean less energy in total. It means more queries.

Per-prompt efficiency crashed toward zero. Total consumption still climbed. That gap is the whole paradox in one chart.

The same pattern shows up in the industry's own disclosures, not just in outside critique. Microsoft's reported emissions have risen roughly 25% year over year in recent filings, with AI datacenter buildout named as a driver. And there's a sharper detail underneath the topline numbers, if you read past the summary slide: "location-based" Scope 2 emissions — the honest accounting, based on what's actually on the local grid — rose somewhere in the range of 92% to 130% for companies like Microsoft and Google, even in years where their "market-based" numbers, built on purchased offsets and renewable certificates, showed emissions declining. Independent reviewers have flagged this gap directly. Offsets are a real accounting instrument, not a fiction, but they answer a different question than "how much power did this actually draw from the grid this year" — and the market-based number is the one that ends up on the slide.

Zoom out to the aggregate and the direction is unambiguous regardless of which accounting method you trust: the IEA puts global datacenter electricity use at roughly 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, projected to reach roughly 945 terawatt-hours by 2030 — more than double, in six years, with AI as the primary driver of the growth curve.

Even the industry's most optimistic voice on this concedes the real version of the argument. Sam Altman has pushed back hard on the viral "17 gallons of water per query" claim, and he's right that the number is fabricated — but he's also said plainly that the underlying concern is legitimate: not the cost of one query, but the total, because the world is now running so many of them. That's a rare moment of a vendor agreeing with the critique instead of just debunking the strawman version of it.

Worth noting who isn't in this conversation at all: Anthropic publishes no sustainability report and makes no efficiency-per-query claims, in contrast to Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI, all of whom publish numbers specifically so they can be measured against them. That's not necessarily worse — a company that publishes no efficiency claim can't be accused of gaming one — but it does mean there's one fewer data point in an industry that badly needs more independently verifiable ones, not fewer.

What actually works is less glamorous than a new chip. Model right-sizing — not defaulting to the frontier model for a task a smaller one handles fine, the same discipline from the second post in this series — is estimated to be able to cut roughly 27.8% of global AI energy use on its own, according to research published in October 2025. Carbon-aware scheduling, running batch and non-urgent workloads when the grid is cleanest, is the other practical lever already documented by the Green Software Foundation and in both AWS's and Google's own infrastructure guidance. Neither of these requires waiting for a hardware breakthrough. Both are available today, to any team willing to treat "which model, and when" as an engineering decision instead of a default.

None of this is an argument to stop using AI, any more than the fuel efficiency of a car is an argument to stop driving. It's an argument against believing the efficiency number is the whole story. Every efficiency gain this industry announces is real, and every one of them is also, by the same paradox, an invitation to use more. The responsible version of "conscious AI use" isn't guilt about the technology — it's the same instinct as post two, pointed at infrastructure instead of individual habits: use the smallest model that gets the job done, and don't mistake a lower price per query for a lower total bill.
