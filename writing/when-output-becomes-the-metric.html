1 — When output becomes the metric

Part of Tokenmaxxing · Farouk Umar

A Meta engineer built an internal leaderboard ranking 85,000 employees by AI token spend. It had a nickname: "Claudeonomics." Andrew Bosworth killed it within days.

Tokenmaxxing is real, it's dated, and it's documented well beyond one awkward internal tool. Meta's own numbers show 60.2 trillion tokens burned company-wide in 30 days — north of $900M if you priced it at retail. Microsoft has run a similar leaderboard since January 2026. Salesforce has publicly displayed minimum AI-spend targets for teams. None of these companies set out to reward volume for its own sake. Token spend is just the number that's already sitting in a billing dashboard, ready to be sorted and ranked, while "did this actually help" requires someone to go and look. Leadership reached for the metric that was already instrumented. They measured the thing that was easy to measure, and got exactly what they measured.

That's Goodhart's law, and it's not a new problem — it's an old one wearing a new badge. Once tokens spent, PRs merged, or lines of code shipped become the number leadership watches, people optimize the number. GitClear's study of 211 million lines of code found exactly that pattern: code duplication up roughly 8x, and the share of commits doing genuine refactoring dropping from about 25% to under 10%. The volume went up. The craft that keeps volume maintainable went down.

The loop closes on itself. The board keeps climbing even as the thing the board was supposed to represent gets worse.

DORA's 2025 survey of roughly 5,000 respondents found the same shape at the industry level: AI adoption correlates with more throughput and less delivery stability. Not a contradiction — the predictable result of optimizing for the half of the equation that's easy to put on a dashboard. The mechanism isn't mysterious. AI makes the writing step nearly free, so more code shows up for review. Review capacity doesn't scale the same way — a senior engineer can only hold so much context in a day — so the bottleneck just moves downstream, from "can we write this" to "can anyone actually verify it," and the second question is the one nobody put on the leaderboard.

The same gap shows up one layer up, in how we choose the model, not just how we measure the humans using it. Every's September 2026 head-to-head found GPT-6 Astra objectively stronger than Claude at writing, at operating software, and at visual design — and still rated Claude ahead on product instincts, the judgment calls about what a task actually needed. Most of that piece sits behind a paywall, so take the topline finding rather than the scorecard: more capable on paper isn't automatically better for the outcome. Capability and judgment are different axes, and a leaderboard only ever measures one of them.

Every's reviewers reached for Astra for writing and prototypes, and kept reaching for Claude for anything with real product stakes.

"Outcomes over outputs" isn't a new insight either. It traces back to the SPACE framework — Forsgren et al., writing in ACM Queue in 2021, well before any of this AI-shaped urgency existed. What's changed is who's saying it and why: it's being re-badged by vendors selling engineering-productivity telemetry, which is a reasonable thing to be skeptical of even when the underlying idea is sound. A vendor dashboard measuring "outcomes" is still a dashboard, and dashboards still get gamed.

Here's the part that will actually force the correction, and it isn't going to be discipline. It's going to be the bill. Budgets are being set per employee and per agent now, and an autonomous scheduled agent burns tokens with nobody watching the leaderboard at all — it just runs, on a cron, at 3am, whether or not the work it produced that week was worth reviewing. A human at least feels the social pressure of a leaderboard. An agent has no such governor, and the org that scaled up autonomous agents fastest without scaling up review capacity to match is the org that gets the biggest bill and the least explanation for what it bought. When the spend line and the delivery-quality line start moving in opposite directions on the same finance report, someone above engineering notices, and the reckoning arrives whether or not the org was ready for it.

That's the thread running through the rest of this series: when to spend tokens on a model at all versus write a script once, what the hardware economics underneath the API price actually look like, what "efficient" AI is quietly costing somewhere else, and why tying your whole workflow to one vendor's rate limits is its own kind of risk. Turbulent water ahead. Get a good guide and a life jacket.
